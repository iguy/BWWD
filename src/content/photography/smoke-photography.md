<p class="lede">When I was a kid in the 70s, the adults smoked while we watched super 8 home movies on the living room wall. The films were bad in every way: dull birthdays and beach trips, shot shaky and out of focus, heads cut off at the top of the frame, everything washed out or lost in shadow. Nobody behind the camera was looking at anything. So I watched the smoke instead, drifting through the projector beam, turning into shapes and then into nothing.</p>

<p>The smoke was better composed than anything on the wall. I didn't know it then, but I was learning to look at something most people never notice: a form that exists for less than a second and can never happen the same way twice.</p>

<h2>Nothing to hold onto</h2>

<p>Smoke is a strange subject. It has no shape of its own, and no colour. You can't pose it, ask it to hold still or try again. Every other photograph I make starts with something that exists, a face or a building, and asks how to see it. Smoke starts with almost nothing.</p>

<p>So everything I do is about the conditions around it. I choose the light and where it falls, the dark behind it, the colour, the one sliver of space where the focus lands. Then I let the smoke do whatever it wants. The work is a collaboration with chance: I set the stage, and the smoke improvises.</p>

<p>What you see on this page isn't smoke so much as light. Smoke only becomes visible where light catches it. Every blue column and amber fold is light I put there, finding a surface for a fraction of a second.</p>

<h2>Figures without a figure</h2>

<p>Look at the tall blue columns and tell me you don't see a body: a torso turning, a hip, a veil falling past a shoulder. I shoot hundreds of frames in a session, and almost all of them are just smoke. I keep the few where it suddenly becomes someone.</p>

<p>That isn't new. Painters spent centuries on drapery studies, learning how cloth folds over a body that isn't there. In the 1890s, Loïe Fuller filled Paris theatres by swirling huge lengths of silk under coloured electric light until she disappeared into the shapes. These photographs live in that same space: the figure suggested, never shown.</p>

<p>This is where the creative decision really happens. Not at the shutter, but in the edit, in choosing which instant out of thousands becomes the picture.</p>

<figure class="wide" style="--ar:1.4993"><img src="/photography/smoke/smoke-2-1024x683.jpg" alt="Violet smoke drifting in wide, overlapping loops" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>

<h2>Holding still what won't</h2>

<p>A macro lens changes the scale. A wisp a few centimetres wide fills the frame like a pillar, a waterfall, a column of light. Something tiny and gone in a breath ends up looking monumental and still.</p>

<p>That stillness is the point. It's the same thing I chase everywhere else, with a rugby player waiting for a pass or two people deep in conversation: the instant just before something changes. With people there's always a story around that moment. With smoke there's nothing else. It's the moment and only the moment, which might be why I keep coming back to it.</p>

<h2>How I shoot it</h2>

<p>A macro lens, strong cross light against a dark background, and a fast shutter speed to freeze the layers as they rise. I set the focus on one small area to pull out the detail and texture, and use noise reduction in post-production to keep the image clean.</p>

<p>The rest is patience, and knowing which frame to keep.</p>
