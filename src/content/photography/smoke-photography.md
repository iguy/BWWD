<p class="lede">When I was a kid in the 70s, the adults smoked while we watched super 8 home movies on the pull-out screen. The films were badly shot, shaky, out of focus, heads cut off at the top of the frame. But that wasn't what I hated about them.</p>

<p>It was the people. The moment anyone stepped in front of the camera, they performed. They pulled funny faces, held a strange fixed smile, popped their eyes wide for the lens. Nobody was natural, because a home movie camera gave everyone time to get ready: time to pretend, time to act out. It all felt pretentious and unreal. Some of the places were genuinely interesting, and they were buried under all that mugging.</p>

<p>So I watched the smoke instead, drifting through the projector beam, turning into shapes and then into nothing. It was the only thing in the room that wasn't putting on a show.</p>

<p>Those evenings taught me more about photography than I realised, what to do and, mostly, what not to do. Nearly everything I photograph now is about people once they've stopped performing. Surrounded by performers and pretenders, I turned to what was real. Smoke is the purest version of that. It can't pose. It doesn't know you're there.</p>

<h2>Nothing to hold onto</h2>

<p>Smoke is a strange subject. It has no shape of its own, and no colour. You can't pose it, ask it to hold still or try again. Every other photograph I make starts with something that exists, a face or a building, and asks how to see it. Smoke starts with almost nothing.</p>

<p>I don't build a studio for it. The light is the winter sun, low and late in the afternoon, racing across a darkened room as it sets. For a few minutes it cuts through at just the right angle. I light the smoke, find the one sliver of space where the focus lands, and let it do whatever it wants. The work is a collaboration with chance, and with a sun that won't wait.</p>

<p>What you see on this page isn't smoke so much as light. Smoke only becomes visible where light catches it. Every blue column and amber fold is that low sun finding a surface for a fraction of a second. Wait a few moments and the angle shifts, the light moves on, and the illusion is gone.</p>

<h2>Figures without a figure</h2>

<p>Look at the tall blue columns and tell me you don't see a body: a torso turning, a hip, a veil falling past a shoulder. I don't shoot thousands of frames to find these. This is it, a handful of images, made in the few minutes the light allowed.</p>

<p>That isn't new. Painters spent centuries on drapery studies, learning how cloth folds over a body that isn't there. In the 1890s, Loïe Fuller filled Paris theatres by swirling huge lengths of silk under coloured electric light until she disappeared into the shapes. These photographs live in that same space: the figure suggested, never shown.</p>

<p>This is where the creative decision really happens: in recognising the shape the instant it appears, and pressing the shutter before it's gone. There's no second take.</p>

<figure class="wide" style="--ar:1.4993"><img src="/photography/smoke/smoke-2-1024x683.jpg" alt="Violet smoke drifting in wide, overlapping loops" loading="lazy" decoding="async"></figure>

<h2>Holding still what won't</h2>

<p>A macro lens changes the scale. A wisp a few centimetres wide fills the frame like a pillar, a waterfall, a column of light. Something tiny and gone in a breath ends up looking monumental and still.</p>

<p>That stillness is the point. Everything here is temporary: the smoke, the light, the form. If you miss it, it's gone. It's the same thing I chase everywhere else, with a rugby player waiting for a pass or two people deep in conversation: the instant just before something changes. With people there's always a story around that moment. With smoke there's nothing else. It's the moment and only the moment, which might be why I keep coming back to it.</p>

<h2>How I shoot it</h2>

<p>A macro lens, natural light from the setting winter sun across a dark room, and a fast shutter speed to freeze the layers as they rise. I set the focus on one small area to pull out the detail and texture, and use noise reduction in post-production to keep the image clean.</p>

<p>The rest is being ready when the light arrives, and knowing it won't stay.</p>
