<p>Most of the people in these photographs aren't looking at me. They're looking at each other: mid-conversation, a hand on a shoulder, a glance down at a child. That's the moment I wait for, when attention leaves the camera and goes to someone else.</p>

<p>A drag stage in London, a rugby sideline, a summer party in the woods, a community day outside the fire station. Different rooms, the same thing happening in all of them. An ongoing series, added as I go.</p>

<div class="split"><figure style="--ar:0.5621"><img src="/photography/people/people-fab16b97.jpg" data-full="/photography/people/people-fab16b97-full.jpg" width="787" height="1400" alt="Man in a blue robe and head wrap sitting by the French Broad River in Marshall, North Carolina" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>Marshall, NC, 2021</figcaption></figure><div class="split-text"><p>A good portrait isn't posed. I watch for the moment someone forgets the camera is there: a turn of the head, a glance toward the river, the way they hold themselves when no one is directing them.</p><p>That's when character shows, and that's the moment I wait for. Whether it's a stranger on the street, a performer on stage or a sitter in the studio, I want the photograph to feel like the person, not the pose.</p></div></div>

What links them is the same patience: waiting until the person stops performing for the camera.</p></div></div>

<p>A lot of these come from community events: Pride, festival processions, a stage at the end of the night. I want them to honour the people who make those spaces, as they actually are. No staging, just the expression that was already there.</p>

<p>Even on stage, I'm not after a face put on for my camera. Performers like these are at home in front of a crowd. I photograph them in the act, not acting for me.</p>
